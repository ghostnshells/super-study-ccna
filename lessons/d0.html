How to learn networking (and anything hard)

The CCNA covers hundreds of facts, dozens of commands and a handful of ideas that take real effort to understand. Most people who struggle with it are not short of intelligence or time; they are using study habits that feel productive but are not. This lesson explains how memory works in plain terms and how every page of this hub is built around it. The ideas come from learning science and are popularized by Barbara Oakley and Terrence Sejnowski in their free course Learning How to Learn.

Two ways your brain works: focused and diffuse

Your brain switches between two modes. In focused mode you concentrate hard on one thing: working a subnetting problem, reading a command output line by line. Focused mode is how you load new material in. In diffuse mode your attention relaxes: walking, showering, staring out of a window. Diffuse mode is when your brain quietly connects the new material to what you already know. It is why the answer to a problem you gave up on sometimes arrives an hour later.

You need both. Hours of nonstop focus with no breaks give the diffuse mode no chance to do its work, and they tire you out. A good session alternates: concentrate, then step away, then come back.

In this hub: lessons are split into short chunks, and every 25 minutes or so of reading the page suggests a real break: get up, move, do not pick up your phone. That break is part of studying, not a pause from it.

Chunks: how experts hold so much in their heads

Your working memory can juggle only about four things at once. Experts get around this by building chunks: tightly connected bundles of ideas that are recalled as a single unit. A beginner sees show ip route output as fifty separate symbols. An experienced engineer sees "an OSPF route, learned from R2, with a metric of 2" in one glance, because each line is a single chunk.

A chunk forms in three steps: focus on the new idea, understand the basic idea (why it works, not just what it is), and then practise it until you can use it without thinking. Understanding alone is not enough; practice is what turns understanding into a chunk you can use under exam pressure.

Chunks also need context: knowing when to use an idea, not just how. That is why this hub starts every lesson with why it comes now and an analogy, and why practice mixes topics together later.

Recall beats re-reading

The single most effective study technique is also the least comfortable: retrieval practice. After reading a section, look away and try to recall the main ideas from memory, in your own words. Then check. The effort of pulling the idea out is what strengthens the memory.

Re-reading and highlighting feel productive because the page looks familiar. That feeling is an illusion of competence: recognizing something when you see it is not the same as being able to produce it on a blank exam screen. Watching a video of someone configuring OSPF is the same trap. The test is always: can I do it, or explain it, with the source closed?

Watch out for: reading a solution and thinking "yes, I would have done that." You did not do it. Close the solution and work the problem yourself; only then compare.

In this hub: every chunk ends with a recall pause, every lesson ends with a blank-page recall before the recap is revealed, and every lesson starts with warm-up questions from earlier lessons.

Spacing and interleaving

Memories fade on a predictable curve, and each time you successfully recall something just as it is starting to fade, it lasts longer. Spaced repetition uses this: review a topic after a day, then a few days, then a week, then a few weeks. A little practice spread over many days beats the same amount crammed into one.

Interleaving means mixing different kinds of problems in one practice session instead of doing twenty of the same kind in a row. It feels harder, and that is the point: the exam does not tell you which topic a question is about, so you must practise choosing the right approach, not just executing a known one.

In this hub: when you mark a lesson as learned, it is scheduled for review after 1, 3, 7, 16 and 35 days. The plan page shows what is due. Unit warm-up quizzes mix questions from all earlier units.

Getting started when you do not feel like it

Procrastination usually starts with a small flash of discomfort at the thought of the task. The trick is to focus on the process, not the product. "Finish OSPF" is a product, and it is daunting. "Do 25 minutes of focused work on the next chunk" is a process, and anyone can do that.

That is the Pomodoro technique: set a 25-minute timer, turn off notifications, work on one thing only, then take a 5-minute break and do something pleasant. The lesson pages include a timer for this. After four rounds, take a longer break.

  • Plan tomorrow's session at the end of today's: write the one chunk or lab you will start with.
  • Study at the same time each day so starting becomes a habit rather than a decision.
  • Do the hardest work when you are freshest; leave flashcards for tired moments.

Sleep, exercise and the exam

Sleep is when your brain consolidates what you learned and clears away the clutter. A night's sleep after studying is part of the studying; an all-nighter before an exam throws away much of what you crammed. Exercise also helps the brain form new connections, and a walk is an excellent diffuse-mode break.

On exam day:

  • Cisco's exam does not let you go back to earlier questions, so pace yourself: roughly a minute per question, with extra for simulations. If a question has you stuck, eliminate what you can, choose the best remaining answer and move on rather than spending five minutes on it.
  • Nervousness is your body preparing to perform. A few slow breaths from the belly before you start calms it down. Reframing helps too: "I am excited" works better than "I am anxious."
  • Before submitting each answer, take a mental step back and check it against the question. Your brain can make a mistake look correct when you are focused on it.

Follow one web request from your laptop to a server

You do not need any background for this lesson. It tells one short story: you open a laptop, join Wi-Fi and load a web page. Along the way it names every major piece of a network. Each bold term gets a full lesson later; for now, aim to follow the story and keep the big picture. You will come back to this picture again and again.

The cast: what is on a home network

Picture a typical home or small office:

  • Endpoints (hosts): your laptop, phone, a printer. They are where data starts and ends.
  • An access point: the radio that lets wireless devices join the wired network.
  • A switch: connects devices inside one network. A home has a small one built in; an office has racks of them.
  • A router: connects your network to other networks, such as your internet provider's.
  • Your internet service provider (ISP) and, beyond it, the internet: thousands of networks joined by routers.
  • Servers: computers that provide a service, such as the web server you want to reach or a DNS server.

The box your provider gave you, usually called a "Wi-Fi router", is actually several devices in one case: a router, a small switch, an access point, a basic firewall and a DHCP server. In business networks these are separate devices, which is why the CCNA treats them separately.

Two kinds of address

Every device on a network has two kinds of address, and the difference is one of the most important ideas in the course.

  • A MAC address is built into the network card, written in hexadecimal like 3c:22:fb:1a:09:7e. It is used only to deliver data across the local link, to the next device. Think of it as the name on an apartment door: perfect for the last few meters, useless for finding the building.
  • An IP address, such as 192.168.1.23, is assigned by the network and has two parts: a network part and a host part. A suffix like /24 says where the line falls: in 192.168.1.23/24 the first three numbers (192.168.1) name the network and the last (23) names the device. Think of it as a street address: the street tells you the neighborhood, the house number tells you the door.

The core idea of networking: switches deliver data inside one network using MAC addresses. Routers move data between networks using IP addresses. Nearly everything in the CCNA is a detail of this sentence.

Step 1: joining the network (DHCP)

When your laptop joins the Wi-Fi it has no IP address yet. It sends a message to everyone on the local network (a broadcast): "Is there a DHCP server? I need an address." The DHCP server, built into your home router, answers with four things:

  1. An IP address for the laptop, for example 192.168.1.23.
  2. A subnet mask, the /24, which tells the laptop which addresses are on its own network.
  3. A default gateway, 192.168.1.1: the router to send anything that is not local.
  4. A DNS server address: who to ask when it needs to turn a name into an address.

Step 2: finding the server (DNS)

You type www.example.com. Computers route by number, not by name, so the laptop asks the DNS server: "What is the IP address of www.example.com?" The DNS server replies with an address, say 203.0.113.10. DNS is the internet's phone book.

Step 3: local or remote? (default gateway and ARP)

The laptop compares 203.0.113.10 with its own network, 192.168.1. They differ, so the server is remote. The laptop does not try to reach it directly; it hands the data to its default gateway, the router.

To hand data to the router on the local network, the laptop needs the router's MAC address. It finds it with ARP: a broadcast asking "Who has 192.168.1.1? Tell me your MAC address." The router replies. Now the laptop knows where to send the data on the local link.

Step 4: wrapping the data (encapsulation)

The browser's request is wrapped in layers, like a letter in envelopes:

  1. The browser writes the request (HTTPS, which is HTTP protected by encryption).
  2. TCP wraps it and adds the port number 443, which says "this is for the web service" on the server.
  3. IP wraps that and adds the source address 192.168.1.23 and the destination 203.0.113.10. This is a packet.
  4. Ethernet or Wi-Fi wraps the packet and adds the laptop's MAC as the source and the router's MAC as the destination. This is a frame.

The radio sends the frame to the access point, the switch delivers it to the router by MAC address, and the router opens the frame to look at the packet inside. Naming these wrapping steps precisely is the subject of the very next lesson.

Step 5: across the internet (routing)

The router looks up the destination IP in its routing table. It has no specific route to 203.0.113.10, so it uses its default route: "everything else goes to the ISP."

Your laptop's address, 192.168.1.23, is a private address; millions of homes use the same one, so it cannot be used on the internet. The router swaps it for its own public address using NAT and remembers the swap so it can reverse it for the reply.

The packet then crosses many routers. Each one takes off the old frame, reads the IP destination, picks the next router and wraps the packet in a new frame. The IP addresses stay the same the whole way; the MAC addresses change at every hop.

Step 6: the reply

The web server sends the page back to your router's public address. Your router reverses the NAT swap and forwards the reply to 192.168.1.23. TCP on your laptop checks that every piece arrived, asks for anything missing to be sent again, and hands the complete page to the browser. All of this typically takes well under a second.

Lab: Find the story on your own computer

Tool: your own computer's terminal or command prompt | Time: ~20 min

No special software needed. On Windows open Command Prompt; on macOS open Terminal; on Linux open a terminal.

  1. Find your IP address, mask, default gateway and DNS server. Windows: ipconfig /all. macOS: ipconfig getifaddr en0, netstat -rn | grep default and scutil --dns | grep nameserver (your Wi-Fi may be en1 instead of en0). Linux: ip addr, ip route and resolvectl status (or cat /etc/resolv.conf).
  2. Ping your default gateway: ping 192.168.1.1, using your gateway's address. On macOS and Linux, stop it with Ctrl+C.
  3. Ask DNS for an address: nslookup www.cisco.com.
  4. Trace the routers between you and a server: tracert www.cisco.com on Windows, traceroute www.cisco.com on macOS or Linux.
  5. Look at your ARP cache: arp -a. Find your gateway's IP address and its MAC address.

Verify:

  • You can write down your four DHCP values: address, mask (or prefix length), gateway and DNS server.
  • The ping to your gateway gets replies, usually in a few milliseconds.
  • nslookup returns one or more IP addresses for www.cisco.com.
  • The traceroute's first hop is your default gateway. Some later hops may show *; that only means those routers do not answer traceroute.
  • Your gateway appears in the ARP cache with a MAC address, because you just talked to it.
What your results mean

If your address starts with 192.168, 10 or 172.16 to 172.31, it is a private address and your router is doing NAT. If it starts with 169.254, your computer did not get an answer from DHCP. The first traceroute hop is the router you send all remote traffic to: your default gateway. The ARP cache shows the IP-to-MAC mappings your computer learned for devices on the local network only; you will never see the web server's MAC address there, because it is not on your network.

Recap

  • Endpoints, access points, switches, routers and servers each have one job; a home "router" combines several of them.
  • A MAC address delivers data to the next device on the local link. An IP address identifies a device's network and host and works end to end.
  • Switches forward inside a network using MAC addresses; routers forward between networks using IP addresses.
  • DHCP gives a host its address, mask, default gateway and DNS server.
  • DNS turns names into IP addresses. ARP finds the MAC address that belongs to a local IP address.
  • Traffic for another network goes to the default gateway. Each router rebuilds the frame; the IP packet inside stays the same (apart from NAT).

Your lab: Packet Tracer and the Cisco command line

You will learn networking mainly by building small networks and breaking them on purpose. This lesson sets up the free simulator used by every lab in the course and teaches the handful of command-line habits you will use thousands of times. Learn them now, while there is nothing else to think about, so that later labs feel like practice on the topic, not a fight with the tool.

Packet Tracer in five minutes

Cisco Packet Tracer is a free network simulator from the Cisco Networking Academy. Create a free account, download it, and work through the short "Getting Started" course if you have never used it. Then:

  • The device bar at the bottom holds routers, switches and end devices. The labs here use a 2911 router, a 2960 switch and generic PCs.
  • Drag a device onto the workspace, then click it. The CLI tab on a router or switch is the command line. On a PC, the Desktop tab has an IP Configuration window and a Command Prompt.
  • To connect devices, open Connections (the lightning-bolt icon). For now, use "Automatically Choose Connection Type"; lesson 1.3 teaches you to choose cables yourself.
  • Link lights turn green when a link is up, amber while a switch port is still starting, and red when it is down.
  • The bottom-right corner switches between Realtime and Simulation mode. Simulation mode lets you step through traffic one event at a time and open each frame to see its headers. You will use it a lot.

The four modes and their prompts

Cisco IOS gives you different powers in different modes, and the prompt always tells you where you are. This is the most important thing to watch while typing.

ModePromptHow you get thereWhat you do there
User EXECRouter>Where you startA few basic show commands, ping
Privileged EXECRouter#enableEvery show command, save, reload, debug
Global configurationRouter(config)#configure terminalSettings for the whole device, such as the hostname
Interface configurationRouter(config-if)#interface GigabitEthernet0/0Settings for one interface, such as its IP address

There are other sub-modes (for example line console 0 takes you to Router(config-line)#), and they work the same way. exit goes up one level. end or Ctrl+Z goes straight back to privileged EXEC. disable drops from privileged back to user EXEC.

Router>enable
Router#configure terminal
Enter configuration commands, one per line.  End with CNTL/Z.
Router(config)#hostname R1
R1(config)#interface GigabitEthernet0/0
R1(config-if)#exit
R1(config)#end
R1#

Notice the prompt change to R1 the instant the hostname command is entered. Configuration changes take effect immediately.

Getting help without leaving the CLI

  • ? lists what you can type next. show ? lists every show command; sh? lists commands that start with "sh".
  • Abbreviations work as long as they are unambiguous: conf t, int g0/0, sh ip int br.
  • Tab completes a partly typed word. The up arrow recalls earlier commands.
  • The error messages are specific. % Invalid input detected at '^' marker. points to the exact word IOS did not understand (very often you are in the wrong mode). % Incomplete command. means something is missing. % Ambiguous command means your abbreviation matches more than one command.

show, do and no

show commands display information and run in privileged EXEC mode. If you are in a configuration mode, put do in front: do show ip interface brief runs the show command without leaving configuration mode.

To undo almost any configuration command, type it again with no in front: no shutdown turns an interface on, no description removes a description, no ip address removes an address.

running-config and startup-config

The configuration you are changing lives in RAM and is called the running-config. It is lost when the device restarts. The configuration loaded at boot is the startup-config, stored in NVRAM. To keep your changes, copy one to the other:

R1#copy running-config startup-config
Destination filename [startup-config]?
Building configuration...
[OK]
! write memory (or just "wr") does the same thing

show running-config shows the current configuration; show startup-config shows what will load at the next reload.

Four defaults that surprise beginners

  1. Router interfaces start shut down. They show "administratively down" until you enter no shutdown. Switch ports, by contrast, are enabled by default.
  2. A typo in EXEC mode freezes the CLI for a while. IOS assumes an unknown word is a hostname and tries to look it up with DNS ("Translating..."). Prevent this with no ip domain-lookup in global configuration.
  3. Log messages interrupt your typing. Under line console 0, logging synchronous reprints your half-typed line after each message.
  4. The console logs you out after 10 minutes idle. In a lab, exec-timeout 0 0 under line console 0 disables that. (In production you would keep a timeout; Unit 8 explains why.)

The command you will run most

show ip interface brief gives one line per interface: its IP address and two status words. The first is the physical/administrative status, the second is the line protocol (whether Layer 2 is working). Both should say up.

R1#show ip interface brief
Interface              IP-Address      OK? Method Status                Protocol
GigabitEthernet0/0     192.168.1.1     YES manual up                    up
GigabitEthernet0/1     unassigned      YES unset  administratively down down
GigabitEthernet0/2     unassigned      YES unset  administratively down down

G0/0 is configured and working. G0/1 and G0/2 have no address and are still shut down. Lesson 1.4 teaches you to read the other combinations.

Binary and hexadecimal for networking

Computers store addresses as bits: ones and zeros. People write them in decimal (IPv4) or hexadecimal (MAC addresses and IPv6) because long strings of bits are hard to read. Subnetting, the next lesson and the most heavily practised skill on the exam, is arithmetic on those bits. If binary feels shaky, subnetting feels like a set of tricks to memorise. Twenty minutes here makes it make sense.

Place values in one octet

An IPv4 address is 32 bits, written as four groups of eight bits called octets. Each bit position in an octet has a value, doubling from right to left:

Bit1st2nd3rd4th5th6th7th8th
Value1286432168421

The decimal number is the sum of the values whose bit is 1. All eight bits on is 128+64+32+16+8+4+2+1 = 255, the largest value an octet can hold. All off is 0.

Binary to decimal

Write the place values above the bits and add the ones that are 1.

  • 11000000 = 128 + 64 = 192
  • 10101000 = 128 + 32 + 8 = 168
  • 00001010 = 8 + 2 = 10

Decimal to binary: the subtraction method

Go left to right through the place values. If the value fits into what is left, write 1 and subtract it; otherwise write 0.

Example, 172:

  1. 128 fits into 172: write 1, 172 − 128 = 44 left.
  2. 64 does not fit into 44: 0.
  3. 32 fits: 1, 44 − 32 = 12 left.
  4. 16 does not fit into 12: 0.
  5. 8 fits: 1, 12 − 8 = 4 left.
  6. 4 fits: 1, 0 left.
  7. 2 and 1: 0, 0.

172 = 10101100. Check: 128 + 32 + 8 + 4 = 172.

A whole IPv4 address

Convert each octet separately and keep the dots:

192.168.10.77
11000000.10101000.00001010.01001101

For 77: 64 fits (13 left), 32 and 16 do not, 8 fits (5 left), 4 fits (1 left), 2 does not, 1 fits: 01001101.

Masks and the AND operation

A subnet mask is a row of 1 bits followed by 0 bits. The 1s mark the network part of the address. /27 means 27 ones: 11111111.11111111.11111111.11100000, which is 255.255.255.224.

To find which network an address is in, a device does a bitwise AND of the address and the mask: each result bit is 1 only if both bits are 1. In the last octet of 192.168.10.77/27:

  77  = 01001101
 224  = 11100000
 AND  = 01000000  = 64

So 192.168.10.77/27 is in network 192.168.10.64. Lesson 1.6 teaches a much faster method that avoids writing out bits, but this is why that method works. When the shortcut confuses you, come back to the bits.

Because a mask is always 1s then 0s, only nine values can ever appear in a mask octet:

00000000011111000248
1000000012811111100252
1100000019211111110254
1110000022411111111255

Hexadecimal: four bits per digit

Hexadecimal (hex) counts in sixteens using the digits 0 to 9 and then A to F for ten to fifteen. One hex digit holds exactly four bits, which is why it is used for long addresses: two hex digits make one byte.

HexDecimalBinaryHexDecimalBinary
000000881000

To convert hex to binary, replace each digit with its four bits. AC = 1010 1100, which is 172 in decimal (A × 16 + C = 160 + 12). To convert binary to hex, split it into groups of four from the right and replace each group.

  • A MAC address is 48 bits = 12 hex digits, for example 0011.2233.4455 in Cisco's format.
  • An IPv6 address is 128 bits = 32 hex digits, written as eight groups of four, for example 2001:0db8:0000:0000:0000:0000:0000:0001. Unit 5 shows how to shorten it.

Exam trap: hex digits are not decimal. In an IPv6 address, the group 0010 is sixteen, not ten.

Practice

Work each one on paper before opening the answers. If you get one wrong, redo it tomorrow.

  1. Convert 11110000 to decimal.
  2. Convert 01111111 to decimal.
  3. Convert 200 to binary.
  4. Convert 99 to binary.
  5. Convert hex FE and 1F to decimal.
  6. Which network is 10.1.1.130/25 in? (Hint: a /25 mask's last octet is 128.)
  7. Write hex 2001 in binary.
Answers
  1. 128 + 64 + 32 + 16 = 240.
  2. 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 127.
  3. 200 − 128 = 72, − 64 = 8, skip 32 and 16, − 8 = 0: 11001000.
  4. Skip 128, 99 − 64 = 35, − 32 = 3, skip 16, 8, 4, − 2 = 1, − 1 = 0: 01100011.
  5. FE = 15 × 16 + 14 = 254. 1F = 16 + 15 = 31.
  6. 130 = 10000010; AND 10000000 = 10000000 = 128. Network 10.1.1.128.
  7. 0010 0000 0000 0001.

Recap

  • An octet's place values are 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1; an octet holds 0 to 255.
  • Binary to decimal: add the place values of the 1 bits. Decimal to binary: subtract place values left to right.
  • IPv4 = 32 bits in four octets. A mask is 1s (network) then 0s (host).
  • Address AND mask = network address.
  • Only nine mask octet values exist: 0, 128, 192, 224, 240, 248, 252, 254, 255.
  • One hex digit = four bits. MAC = 48 bits = 12 hex digits; IPv6 = 128 bits = 32 hex digits.
